The Three Pillars of ZKP

Every zero knowledge proof must satisfy these three properties

✅

Completeness

If the statement is true, an honest prover can always convince an honest verifier.

Example If you actually know the password, you'll always be able to prove it.
🛑

Soundness

If the statement is false, no cheating prover can convince the verifier (except with tiny probability).

Example If you don't know the password, you can't fake a proof — you'd need astronomical luck.
🔮

Zero-Knowledge

The verifier learns absolutely nothing beyond the fact that the statement is true.

Example The verifier knows you have the password, but can't figure out what it is.

The Color Blind Verifier

Prove you can distinguish two colors — without telling the verifier which is which

🎨 How It Works

  1. You (Prover) see two balls — one red and one green
  2. The Verifier is color-blind — they look identical to them
  3. The Verifier hides the balls behind their back
  4. They may or may not swap them, then show you
  5. You must say whether they swapped or not
  6. If you can do this many times, you've proven you see colors!
Zero Knowledge: The verifier never learns which ball is red and which is green — only that you can tell them apart.

Hash Commitment Proof

Prove you know a secret number without revealing it — using cryptographic hashes

🔢 How It Works

  1. You choose a secret number
  2. You compute its SHA-256 hash — a unique fingerprint
  3. You send the hash to the verifier (this is your commitment)
  4. The verifier challenges: "Prove you know a number with this hash"
  5. You reveal the number — the verifier hashes it and compares
Key Insight: SHA-256 is a one-way function. Given the hash, nobody can figure out your original number. But given the number, anyone can verify the hash matches.

Schnorr Identification Protocol

A real cryptographic ZKP — prove you know a secret key using modular arithmetic

🔐 The Schnorr Protocol

This is one of the most fundamental ZKP protocols in cryptography.

1
Setup: Public parameters: prime p, generator g. Secret key: x. Public key: y = g^x mod p
2
Commitment: Prover picks random r, sends t = g^r mod p
3
Challenge: Verifier sends random challenge c
4
Response: Prover computes s = r + c·x mod (p-1)
5
Verify: Check that g^s ≡ t · y^c (mod p)
Why is this Zero-Knowledge? The verifier sees (t, c, s) but can't extract x because r is random and unknown. Any (t, c, s) triple could have been generated without knowing x!
